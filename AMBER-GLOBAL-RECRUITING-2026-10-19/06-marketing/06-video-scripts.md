# Video scripts

Six scripts, each about two to three minutes spoken. The text under each heading is exactly what is said; the "On screen" list is not spoken.

## Production notes

- **Who speaks.** The scripts are in the owner's voice, the same "I" as the emails. If the owner would rather not be on camera, a voiceover with screen recordings works, and the voice should be the owner's own. If a synthetic voice or an avatar is used instead, say so in the first line of the description and turn on YouTube's setting that labels altered or synthetic content. The scripts do not claim anything a person could not truthfully say about themselves, so they work either way.
- **Where they live.** A new channel for Amber Global Recruiting, created by the owner. The existing AmberOne channel is aimed at students, and the audiences do not mix. Creating a channel is an external account action and needs the owner.
- **Where viewers go.** The scripts end on "the address is in the description". A website is not needed yet. When there is a page, it should carry the same plain description as the one-page offer.
- **Music, footage and pictures.** Only material the owner has the right to use. Screen recordings of the owner's own page should be checked first for anything that is not meant to be public (addresses, reply text, money).
- **Order.** Start with the cold-email checklist (it rests on published rules, not on experience) and the notebook method for long-open roles (it says plainly that it is the method behind a service being started). The first-call video says that its speaker has not made those calls yet; record it only if the owner is comfortable saying that. The two "later" scripts describe a running system and are recorded only when every sentence in them is true. If the aim is trust rather than teaching, the "later" videos (how the list is made, the weekly report) are the honest centerpiece, and they need the system to have run.
- **After publishing.** Reply to every comment yourself. If someone asks for a sample, treat it as a positive reply on the page.

### The job that stays posted: a free way for small agencies to find clients

*Status: can be used once the owner has approved the process.* A three-minute teaching video for staffing agency owners. It teaches the method with a notebook first and mentions the list at the end.

Before it is used:
- the process is approved
- the owner decides who speaks it and whether a synthetic voice or picture is used (see the production notes)

> Here is a way to find employers to call that needs only a notebook. It is the method behind a small research service I am starting.
>
> Open a company's careers page and write down one role. Come back in six weeks. If the same role is still there, somebody inside that company has a reason to want help. It might be a stretched recruiter, an offer that is too low, or an earlier agency that did not work out. You will not know which. But you do have a reason to call, and you know what to say.
>
> Compare two openers. The first: "We provide staffing solutions." The second: "Your accounts payable role has been listed since early September, and I work with accounting candidates." Only the second shows the hiring manager you looked.
>
> A few habits make this work. Use pages anyone can open. Write down the date the page gives, and if it gives none, write the day you first saw the role and mark it that way. Never guess an email address. If the company prints a hiring address, use it. If it does not, use the contact form or the main phone line.
>
> And a few things to leave out. Do not say the role is hard to fill, because you do not know that. All you know is that it is still listed. Do not write as if you know the owner. Do not promise anything about candidates you have not met.
>
> I have built a small program that does this research and puts it in a weekly list for small agencies. It is new and I have no other clients yet, so the first sample is free, and the address is in the description. If it is not for you, the method still works with a notebook and an hour a week.

*308 words. Wording check: 0 errors, 0 warnings.*

**Video description:** A notebook method for finding employers worth calling: look for roles that stay posted. It cannot tell you why a role is still open, only that it is. A sample of the list is free; write to the address below.

**On screen:**
- Open on a careers page with one role highlighted.
- Show a notebook with a role and a date written down.
- Show the two openers side by side as text on screen.
- End on the sample sheet with a few rows, company names visible, no addresses.

### How the list is made, including what does not work yet

*Status: wait until the system has run (conditions below).* A build-in-public video for agency owners who ask where the list comes from. Record it only after the first live read, and change any sentence that has stopped being true.

Before it is used:
- the lane has made its first successful live read
- every statement below is still true on the day of recording

> This is how the list I send to staffing agencies is made, including the parts that do not work yet.
>
> Every day a program reads the public job boards of the companies on its list. These are pages you could open yourself. It writes down each role it finds, with the date the page shows, or the day it first saw the role if the page shows none.
>
> After 45 days, a role that is still listed goes on the long-open list. Recruiter jobs are left off, since a staffing firm's own openings are not what an agency wants to see.
>
> Then the program looks on the company's own website for a hiring address, something like jobs or careers, printed where anyone can read it. It checks the site's rules for automated visitors first, and it stops if the site asks not to be contacted. It does not guess addresses. It does not keep names of people.
>
> Now the part that is not finished. I have only just started and I have no customers. Some job boards publish their data in formats I have only read about, so a few of them may fail in the first weeks. When one does, the program reports it instead of making something up.
>
> Nothing leaves my desk without me. The program drafts a note, I read it, I change it, and I send it myself. If you receive one, you can tell me to stop, and I will.
>
> If you run a small agency and want to see what a row looks like, the sample is free.

### Five checks before you send a cold email to a company in the US

*Status: can be used once the owner has approved the process.* A teaching video for anyone doing outreach. It does not mention Amber's product except in the description.

Before it is used:
- the process is approved
- the owner is comfortable saying "I am not a lawyer" on camera and means it

> If you write to companies you have never spoken to, these five checks are the ones I use. I am not a lawyer, so take them as a checklist and not as advice.
>
> One. Say who you are. A real name, a real business, and a postal address in the message. A post office box counts if it is registered with the Postal Service.
>
> Two. Make the subject say what the message is. If it is a sales note, do not dress it as a reply to a conversation that never happened.
>
> Three. Give a way out, and mean it. One line is enough: reply "no thanks" and I will not write again. Then keep your word. The US rule gives you ten business days. I would do it the same day.
>
> Four. Write to an address the company published itself. A hiring or contact address on its own website is one thing. An email guessed from a person's name is another.
>
> Five. Check where the person is. The United States is the easy case. Outside it the rules differ, and in several countries they ask for consent first, so check before you write there.
>
> Everything else is about being decent. Send few messages, send them by hand, and write each one for the person who will read it.
>
> And before you use an address, ask where it came from, because you are the one who sends the message.

*239 words. Wording check: 0 errors, 0 warnings.*

**Video description:** A plain checklist for cold email to companies in the United States. It is not legal advice; ask a lawyer about your own case. The rules differ in other countries.

**On screen:**
- Five short title cards, one per check.
- Show an example footer with a made-up name and address.
- On the last check show a spreadsheet column headed "where I found this address".

### What software should and should not do with a resume

*Status: can be used once the owner has approved the process.* A teaching video for small employers. It explains the principle behind Amber's own candidate sheet without selling anything.

Before it is used:
- the process is approved
- the owner has checked the sentence about New York City, Illinois, Colorado and California against the lawyer's current advice before publishing

> More employers are using software to read applications. Some of what it does is useful. Some of it is a legal problem waiting to happen.
>
> Here is the line I would draw. Software can read a resume and pull out what the person wrote about the things the job needs. "Five years of payroll work, says they used ADP." Or: "Does not say whether they have run month-end close." That saves a person ten minutes per application, and the person can check every line against the original.
>
> What software should not do is score people or put them in order. A score looks objective and is not. It carries whatever was in the data it learned from, including things you would never use on purpose, like a gap in someone's work history that has nothing to do with the job.
>
> There are rules about this already. New York City requires a bias audit and a notice for some tools. Illinois, Colorado and California have their own. Which ones apply to you depends on what the tool does and where the applicant lives, so ask someone qualified before you buy anything.
>
> A person decides who gets an interview, and a person can say why. Use software to make reading faster, not to make the decision. If you are shopping for a tool, ask the vendor one question: for each line of your summary, can you show me the sentence the applicant wrote?
>
> I am not offering this yet. If I do, my summaries will quote the applicant and say "not stated" when something is missing.

*264 words. Wording check: 0 errors, 0 warnings.*

**Video description:** Where software helps with applications and where it becomes a legal and fairness problem. Not legal advice; the rules depend on the tool and on where the applicant lives.

**On screen:**
- Show an invented resume with two highlighted lines.
- Show a sheet with the columns requirement, what the applicant wrote, and "not stated".
- Show a ranked list with scores crossed out.

### How I would open a first call about a role that has been open too long

*Status: can be used once the owner has approved the process.* A teaching video for newer recruiters and agency owners. It says plainly that the speaker has not made these calls yet and makes no claim about Amber.

Before it is used:
- the process is approved

> You found a role that has been up for two months, and now you have to say something. I have not made these calls myself yet, so treat this as a place to start.
>
> Start with what you saw, not with who you are. "Hi, I noticed the operations manager role on your site has been up since August." That sentence is true, it takes four seconds, and it shows you did your homework.
>
> Then ask. Do not tell. "Is that role still open, or have you stopped chasing it?" It is an easy question to answer honestly, because the open role is a worry to them and nobody has asked.
>
> If they say it is still open, ask what they have tried. Job boards, referrals, another agency. People like to explain what they have already done, and you will learn what not to repeat.
>
> If they say it is closed, thank them, ask whether any other role is giving them trouble, and stop there. Do not push.
>
> What not to say: that you have the perfect candidate, that you can fill it in a week, or that nobody else could do it as well. You do not know any of that yet, and the person on the other end has heard it before.
>
> If the call goes well, offer one small thing you can do this week, and then do it. A reputation in a small market is made of small things done when you said you would do them.

*251 words. Wording check: 0 errors, 0 warnings.*

**Video description:** A plain way to open a first call or email about a role you found posted for weeks. The speaker has not made these calls yet, so treat it as a place to start. The examples are invented.

**On screen:**
- Show a careers page with a role highlighted and the date circled.
- Show the opening line as text on screen.
- Show three "do not say" lines crossed out.

### The weekly report, read straight off the dashboard

*Status: wait until the system has run (conditions below).* A short recurring video. The numbers are read from the screen, never written into the script in advance.

Before it is used:
- the lane has run for a full week
- the owner reads the numbers off the page without rounding them up

> Here is the weekly report for Amber Global Recruiting. I read it straight off the dashboard, and I do not round in my own favor.
>
> First, what the software did. It read the public job boards on its list. The screen shows how many roles it saw, how many have now been open for 45 days or more, and how many companies that is.
>
> Second, what I did. I read the notes the software drafted, changed the ones I did not like, and sent the ones I was happy with. The screen shows how many I sent and how many I threw away.
>
> Third, what came back. Replies, good and bad, and anyone who asked me to stop. I keep every request to stop for good, and that number is on the screen too.
>
> Fourth, money. What came in, what went out, and the proof. For something to count as income there has to be a bank deposit or a payment record I can point to. If the answer is zero, I will say so.
>
> Then the part I care about most. What broke, what I changed, and what I will try next week.
>
> If you are building something similar, the order matters. Count what you did before you count what you hope will happen.

*215 words. Wording check: 0 errors, 0 warnings.*

**Video description:** Weekly numbers for Amber Global Recruiting, read from the dashboard: what the software did, what I sent, what came back, what came in and went out.

**On screen:**
- Screen recording of the owner's page, top to bottom: numbers, sources panel, money.
- Pause on each number long enough to read it.
- If a number is zero, read it out as zero.
